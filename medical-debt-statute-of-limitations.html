Medical debt statute of limitations, explained

A statute of limitations is the deadline for suing over a debt. It varies by state and by type of debt, so check yours before you pay, sign, or ignore anything.

The basics

Most states set time limits for debt lawsuits, commonly in the range of three to six years, and some longer. Which limit applies can depend on whether the debt is treated as a written contract, an open account, or another category. Look up your state's rule in its civil statutes or ask a legal aid office.

What "time-barred" does and doesn't mean

  • The debt is not erased

    A time-barred debt may still be owed, and collectors can generally still ask you to pay.

    Still owed

  • Suing may be off the table

    A collector generally cannot successfully sue, or threaten to sue, over a debt that is past the limit. If you are sued anyway, raise the time limit in your written answer; the court will not do it for you.

    Defense

  • The clock can restart

    In many states, a payment or a written acknowledgment can restart the clock. Do not pay or sign anything on an old debt until you know your state's rule.

    Be careful

  • Credit reports run on a separate clock

    Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, most negative items can stay on a credit report for seven years from the first delinquency, regardless of the lawsuit deadline.

    Separate

Before you worry about the deadline

If the bill is from a nonprofit hospital, you may be able to apply for financial assistance for up to 240 days after the first bill, and collection actions are limited while an application is pending. Applying first can reduce or erase the balance. See how it works and the wage garnishment guide.

If you are sued

Do not ignore a court summons. Respond before the deadline on the paperwork and contact legal aid. Missing it can lead to a default judgment.
