Deep dive

How hospital charity care actually works

Last reviewed November 2026 · Based on IRS and Treasury rules (sources at the bottom)

Most people never learn that many hospitals are legally required to offer free or discounted care, or that unpaid bills can't be sent to collections right away. This page explains the federal rules behind hospital financial assistance, how your discount is actually calculated, the deadlines that protect you, and how to apply.

Key numbers Legal foundation Who qualifies The AGB rule Presumptive eligibility Collection timeline Step by step Hospital types Common mistakes
The short version

Four numbers worth remembering

If you only have a minute, these are the protections that matter most when a bill arrives from a nonprofit hospital:

120 days

Minimum wait before collections

After your first post-discharge bill, a nonprofit hospital generally can't start "extraordinary" collection actions like credit reporting or lawsuits for at least 120 days.

30 days

Written warning first

The hospital must send written notice at least 30 days before such actions, naming them and giving you a deadline to apply for assistance.

240 days

Your application window

You have at least 240 days from your first post-discharge bill to apply, and you can apply as early as the day you receive care.

AGB

A cap on what you can be charged

If you qualify, you can't be billed more than "amounts generally billed" to insured patients, and often you'll owe far less or nothing.

The legal foundation

IRS Section 501(r): the rule that makes charity care mandatory

Since the Affordable Care Act, every hospital facility run by a 501(c)(3) nonprofit must satisfy four requirements under IRS Section 501(r), applied facility by facility, or risk losing its tax-exempt status. That tax exemption is worth a great deal to a hospital, which is why the rules have teeth. Two of the four requirements shape your bill directly.

01

Community Health Needs Assessment

A formal assessment of community health needs at least every 3 years, with a public plan for addressing them.

02

Financial Assistance Policy

A written, board-adopted financial assistance policy (FAP) and emergency care policy, publicized and applied consistently.

03

Limit on amounts charged

FAP-eligible patients can't be charged more than the "amounts generally billed" (AGB) for emergency or other medically necessary care, and never full "gross charges."

04

Billing & collections limits

The hospital must make reasonable efforts to find out whether you qualify for assistance before taking extraordinary collection actions.

A FAP isn't just a brochure. Under Treasury Regulation §1.501(r)-4, it has to spell out in writing:

  • Who is eligible, and whether that means free care, discounted care, or both
  • The basis for calculating what an eligible patient will be charged
  • How to apply, and what documentation is required
  • What the hospital does if a bill goes unpaid, including the actions it may take
  • Which doctors and provider groups working at the hospital are covered by the policy, and which are not

It also has to be widely publicized: posted in full on the hospital's website, available as free paper copies (including at the emergency department and admissions), accompanied by a plain-language summary, and translated into any language spoken by the lesser of 1,000 people or 5% of the community the hospital serves.

Who qualifies

Eligibility is set by each hospital, within federal guardrails

Federal law requires a FAP but does not set the income cutoff. Each hospital chooses its own, so two nearby hospitals can offer very different help. Most policies work like this:

  • Income, usually as a percentage of the Federal Poverty Level (FPL). A common structure is free care below one threshold, then a sliding-scale discount up to a higher one. The exact cutoffs are in the hospital's own policy.
  • Household size. The same income goes further, or less far, depending on how many people it supports.
  • Sometimes assets, residency, or both. These vary a lot. Check the policy before assuming you're out.
  • Insured patients can qualify too. Many policies explicitly cover people with high deductibles or coinsurance. Being insured doesn't automatically disqualify you.
  • Medically necessary care. Elective and cosmetic care is typically excluded.

Want a quick read on where you might land? Try our free eligibility estimate, or look up your hospital's published policy.

The AGB rule

"Amounts Generally Billed": how your price cap is calculated

A hospital's sticker prices (its "chargemaster" or gross charges) bear little relation to what insurers actually pay. The AGB rule stops hospitals from billing eligible patients off that inflated number. The hospital must pick one of two methods, and name it in its FAP:

MethodHow it works
Look-backOnce a year, the hospital divides the allowed amounts on claims from the prior 12 months by the gross charges for those same claims. That gives an "AGB percentage" applied to your bill. The claims counted can be Medicare fee-for-service alone, Medicare plus private health insurers, or those combined with Medicaid. Allowed amounts include the patient's share (copays, coinsurance, deductibles), not just the insurer's payment.
ProspectiveThe hospital works out what Medicare (and/or Medicaid) would have allowed for your specific care, including your share as a patient, and uses that amount as the cap.

Two details that surprise people. First, the AGB cap applies to emergency and other medically necessary care; for any other care the FAP covers, the hospital must still charge you less than gross charges. Second, hospitals get up to 120 days after each 12-month period ends to start using their updated AGB percentage, so the number on your bill may lag slightly.

A worked example (hypothetical numbers)

Imagine a $20,000 hospital bill and a hospital whose AGB percentage is 30%. This is illustrative only; real percentages differ by hospital and year.

ScenarioYou'd oweWhy
No assistance applied$20,000The full gross charge, which is what you'd be billed if you never apply.
Eligible, AGB cap onlyUp to $6,00030% of $20,000. This is the ceiling for any FAP-eligible patient.
Eligible for a deeper discountLess than $6,000The hospital's income tiers apply on top of the AGB cap.
Eligible for free care$0Your income falls in the hospital's free-care tier.

AGB is a ceiling, not your final price. If you also qualify for free or further-discounted care under the hospital's income tiers, that applies on top of it, and usually lands well below the AGB amount.

Presumptive eligibility

Sometimes you don't have to apply at all

Hospitals may decide you're eligible using information they already have or can get elsewhere, with no application from you. Whether they do this, and on what basis, has to be described in their FAP. Examples of what hospitals commonly rely on include enrollment in means-tested programs such as Medicaid, SNAP, or WIC, or screening done by a third-party eligibility vendor.

There is a catch. If the hospital presumes you qualify for less than its most generous assistance, it must tell you the basis for that decision, explain how to apply for more, and give you a reasonable time to do so before it takes extraordinary collection actions. A presumptive approval can be a floor, not a ceiling, so it's worth checking whether you'd qualify for more.

Billing & collections

The clocks that protect you

Federal rules keep nonprofit hospitals from rushing straight to collections. Here is the order of events, measured from your first post-discharge billing statement (the first bill you receive after care):

  • You receive care

    The application period opens now. You don't have to wait for a bill to apply.

    Day 0

  • First post-discharge bill

    Both clocks below start here. The hospital must also tell you that financial assistance exists and how to apply.

    Bill #1

  • At least 120 days pass

    The hospital must wait this long before starting extraordinary collection actions, and only after making reasonable efforts to check whether you qualify.

    120 days

  • Final written notice

    At least 30 days before such an action, you must get written notice naming the action, a plain-language FAP summary, and a deadline to apply. The hospital must also make a reasonable effort to tell you orally.

    30+ days before

  • Application period ends

    Not before 240 days after your first post-discharge bill, and not before the deadline given in the final notice, whichever is later.

    240 days

  • You submit an application

    Collection actions must pause while it's reviewed. If it's incomplete, the hospital must tell you in writing what's missing and give you a reasonable time to fix it.

    Any time

What counts as an "extraordinary collection action" (ECA)?

The rules are strictest for these steps, so they're the ones to watch for on any letter you receive:

  • Selling your debt to a collector (with narrow exceptions)
  • Reporting the debt to credit bureaus
  • Delaying or denying medically necessary care, or demanding payment first, because of unpaid prior bills
  • Legal or judicial action: placing a lien, foreclosing on real estate, seizing bank accounts or other property, filing a lawsuit, or garnishing wages

Ordinary billing statements, reminder letters, and phone calls are not ECAs, so you may receive those during the waiting period. If you do get a threatening letter, don't ignore it: it likely contains the deadline that matters most.

Step by step

From hospital bill to approved application

  1. Get care first

    Emergency and other medically necessary care is provided regardless of your ability to pay. Eligibility for assistance is decided afterward.

  2. Request an itemized bill

    Check it for duplicate charges or services you didn't receive. Hold off on paying until you've looked into assistance.

  3. Find the hospital's FAP

    It must be posted on the hospital's website, usually under "Billing," "Financial Assistance," or "Patient Resources." Check which providers it covers.

  4. Get the application and plain-language summary

    Both are free, available online, by mail, and in person at the emergency department and admissions desk.

  5. Gather your documents

    Typically recent pay stubs, a tax return, or a benefits award letter. Hospitals generally can't deny you for lacking documents that the policy or application form doesn't ask for.

  6. Submit, and keep proof

    Apply within your application period, earlier is better, especially if collection notices have started. Keep a copy, and note the date and how you sent it.

  7. The hospital reviews it

    It may ask for more information. If your application is incomplete, it must tell you in writing what's missing.

  8. Get a written decision

    An approval should state your discount tier and new balance. If you paid more than you owe as an eligible patient, the hospital must refund the excess (amounts under $5 may be excluded).

  9. Follow up or appeal

    If you're approved for less than expected, ask in writing for a re-review and send any extra documents. Many policies allow this, and a partial approval often isn't final. See what to do if your application is denied.

Not every hospital is the same

Nonprofit, for-profit, and government hospitals play by different rules

Hospital typeWhat's required
Nonprofit (501(c)(3))A written FAP under IRS 501(r) is mandatory to keep tax-exempt status. Most U.S. community hospitals fall here, and everything above applies.
For-profitNo federal 501(r) mandate, so assistance is voluntary unless your state requires it. Some states impose charity-care or discount rules on all hospitals regardless of tax status. Check your state guide.
Government / publicOften covered by separate state or county indigent-care laws and funding programs. 501(r) applies if the hospital also holds 501(c)(3) status.

One more distinction: EMTALA (the Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act) requires Medicare-participating hospitals with an emergency department to screen and stabilize you regardless of insurance or ability to pay. That is separate from charity care. EMTALA guarantees the care happens; the hospital's FAP determines what you'll ultimately be billed for it.

Common mistakes

Mistakes that cost patients money

  • Paying before asking

    Overpayments can be refunded once you're approved, but that's slower and less certain than applying first.

    Avoidable

  • Putting it on a medical credit card

    These often carry deferred-interest terms that can backfire, and they don't reduce what you actually owed.

    Avoidable

  • Assuming insurance disqualifies you

    Many policies explicitly cover insured patients facing high deductibles or coinsurance.

    Common myth

  • Misreading the deadline

    The 240-day clock runs from your first post-discharge bill, not your discharge date, and the deadline in a final notice can extend it.

    Avoidable

  • Ignoring separate doctor bills

    ER physicians, radiologists, and anesthesiologists often bill separately and may not be covered by the hospital's FAP. The policy lists which providers are covered, so check each bill.

    Check the FAP

  • Not appealing a low offer

    A presumptive or partial approval usually isn't final. You can typically submit more documentation.

    Avoidable

  • Tossing collection letters

    The final notice contains your last deadline and names the actions the hospital plans to take.

    Avoidable
